I am passionate about teaching and have taken every opportunity to balance science education with the research responsibilities of my doctoral thesis and postdoc. I'm happy to have had several chances to teach in the college classroom, from undergraduate freshmen in first-year seminars to running the labs of a graduate-level animal behavior course. Below, you can find selections of lectures, activities, notes, and some exemplary student work from the courses I've taught.

* [FYS 191 Syllabus](fys_191_fa23_syllabus.pdf) // A syllabus crafted for a first-year seminar, following principles of inclusive course design & syllabus writing.

* [Lab 7 Reading Guides](lab_7_reading_guides.pdf) // For an advanced animal behavior course, I led a "jigsaw" style discussion where students selected one paper from a series, read it independently, discussed it with folks who read the same paper, and then shared a summary with classmates who selected other papers. To best facilitate discussion, I created this guide to help students navigate each paper while they read independently, or as they discussed in class. 

* ***Parenting in the Animal Kingdom Lesson*** // This is a lesson I created for an introductory animal behavior course focusing on parenting behaviors in the animal kingdom - why animals parent, and the types of ways animals parent. For the latter theme, I wanted to cover the many examples of animal parenting behavior in an engaging and a time-sensitive manner, so I asked students to do a *Bluff the Listener* activity in the style of the NPR comedy show *Wait, Wait Don't Tell Me!*. 

  * [ACTIVITY WORKSHEET](wwdtm_activity.pdf) // The aim of this activity is to help students explore the many kinds of parenting behavior in the animal kingdom. In summary, the activity asks students to split into teams. In summary:
    * Each team is given a unique set of descriptions depicting animal parenting behavior (see below). One of the descriptions is real. 
    * Each team decides what they think is the true behavior. They then read their descriptions to the class, with slight encouragement to convince their classmates to choose the false examples. 
    * The true behavior is revealed. The neat thing about this activity is that both of the "false" behaviors are true animal behaviors, just exaggerated or assigned to the wrong species to make them inaccurate. I make sure to walk students through how  the "false" behaviors are inaccurately portrayed.
    * If all goes well, students now have been informed of 12 types of behaviors that can be considered "parenting" in animals. You can see how this activity played out in class through the lecture notes (below).

  * [LECTURE & ANNOTATED NOTES](week_8_n.pdf) // The lecture slides, annotated. Black boxes depict topics we discussed verbally in class. Generally, I make the un-annotated version available to students before class, and an annotated version after.

  * **BIO 550 Final Projects** // As the lab instructor for an advanced animal behavior course, I was tasked to walk students through a final project where they designed their own manipulative experiments or observations of lab-reared or wild animals. The project was an involved process where groups met one-on-one with me to approve a project design. At the end of the semester, students summarized their work in the format of a research poster. They then presented their work in a poster session where they could share their hard work with their classmates.
